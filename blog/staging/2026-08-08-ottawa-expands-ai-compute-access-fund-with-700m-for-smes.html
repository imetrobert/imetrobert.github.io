Executive Perspective

From Robert's Desk

Many executives I speak with are still focused on AI as a technology problem. They task their CIOs with "getting AI done," assuming the biggest hurdles are technical integration or model selection. This perspective misses the real challenge: AI is a governance problem first, and a commercial problem second. We are watching the market reprice the cost of AI in real-time. Microsoft’s move to cap internal token spending is not about innovation; it is about the harsh reality of operational expenditure. The notion that AI is "free" once the software is acquired is a dangerous fantasy. Enterprises must confront the true, ongoing consumption costs of these models, which behave more like utilities than traditional software licenses.

The real surprise this month is not a new model, but the explicit government focus on AI transparency. Ottawa's consultation on detecting AI-generated content and understanding AI systems is a direct response to public trust concerns. This is a crucial signal for every Canadian business. If you are deploying AI that interacts with customers or generates content, you must be able to explain how it works and what data it used. "Black box" AI deployments are a compliance risk, especially under Quebec Law 25, which has already established a high bar for automated decision transparency. Ignoring these regulatory signals is a strategic error.

The organizations that will win are those that treat AI not as a magic bullet for every problem, but as a set of tools with specific costs, risks, and governance requirements. This demands a shift from pilot-centric thinking to a focus on scalable, compliant deployment. The hardest part is often not the technology, but getting different business units to agree on common data definitions, risk tolerances, and clear accountability for AI outputs. Without this internal alignment, even the most advanced models will stall at the proof-of-concept stage, never delivering real business outcomes.

Looking Ahead

These are predictions, not reported facts — Robert’s assessment of where this goes next, offered so you can judge it against your own.

One month

I expect the initial enthusiasm for unconstrained AI agents will give way to a sharper focus on agent governance and security, driven by early, public failures.

Six months

My assessment is that Canadian businesses will confront the true, escalating operational costs of AI, leading to a wave of internal cost-optimization initiatives and a more disciplined approach to model selection.

One year

I think it is likely that provincial privacy regulators, particularly in Quebec, will issue their first enforcement actions related to AI transparency and automated decision-making.
