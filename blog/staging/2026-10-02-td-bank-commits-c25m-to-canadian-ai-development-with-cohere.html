Executive Perspective

From Robert's Desk

The "all in" conference in San Francisco last week, where I spent three days, highlighted a critical shift: the AI conversation has moved from "what if" to "what now." The noise around generative AI is settling into a clearer signal about agentic systems. We are no longer debating whether AI can understand; the focus is now on whether it can act on its own, and how to manage that autonomy. This is a profound change for Canadian businesses, especially those in regulated sectors like financial services.

What worries me most is the executive tendency to delegate AI governance solely to IT or legal. The Anthropic warnings about "existential risks" are not hypothetical; they are a stark reminder that these systems can behave unpredictably. Relying on a technical team to manage ethical and societal risks is a fundamental misunderstanding of the challenge. This requires C-suite leadership, embedding risk management and ethical oversight directly into strategic planning, not as an afterthought.

The biggest opportunity many Canadian organizations are overlooking is the integration of AI agents into existing enterprise workflows. The new offerings from OpenAI and Meta are not just consumer toys; they are powerful tools for automating complex, multi-step processes. However, the immediate mistake will be piloting these without clear guardrails. Without a robust framework for human review and intervention, these agents will create more problems than they solve, particularly in sensitive areas like customer data or financial transactions. The organizations that win will be those that prioritize secure, auditable deployment.
