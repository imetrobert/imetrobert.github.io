Executive Perspective

From Robert's Desk

The federal government’s AI Compute Access Fund, updated this month, is a welcome and necessary intervention. It directly addresses the second-largest cost for any serious AI builder: compute. For too long, Canadian companies have either offshored their compute or struggled with exorbitant domestic pricing. A two-thirds subsidy for Canadian-hosted compute changes that equation entirely, making it economically viable to keep sensitive training and inference workloads on Canadian soil. This is a clear signal that Ottawa understands the strategic importance of sovereign AI infrastructure.

What consistently surprises me is how organizations treat data residency. Many executives still view it as a legal or compliance problem, something to be 'delegated' to a privacy officer or external counsel. Google's updated data residency for Generative AI this month shows this is now a core technical and architectural decision. If your enterprise is not actively designing its AI deployments with data sovereignty in mind, you are building in future technical debt and regulatory exposure. The default assumption should be local, secure, and auditable.

The biggest risk executives are overlooking right now is the operationalization of AI agents. OpenAI's new ChatGPT Work and desktop app are not just smarter chatbots; they are autonomous systems capable of acting across your enterprise data and applications. The conversation around security often stops at data at rest or in transit. Agentic AI introduces data in use – the decisions an agent makes, the data it accesses, the actions it takes. This requires a shift from perimeter defense to continuous monitoring of agent behaviour and outputs. The failure mode is a compromised agent operating silently within your network, not a breach of a database.

This is not a theoretical problem. I have seen organizations deploy pilot AI systems with enthusiastic business unit sponsorship, only for them to stall at scale because the underlying data governance or security frameworks were never designed for autonomous operation. The technology moves faster than the policy. We must build the guardrails concurrently, not as an afterthought. This means demanding explicit transparency from vendors on agent capabilities and audit trails, and investing in internal expertise that understands agentic risk.
