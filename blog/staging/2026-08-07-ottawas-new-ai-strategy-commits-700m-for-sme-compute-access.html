Executive Perspective

From Robert's Desk

This month, the sheer scale of investment in AI infrastructure surprised me. Amazon alone raised its 2026 capital expenditure to $220 billion, yet still expects capacity constraints into 2028. What we're seeing on the ground is that the race for compute is far from over. This directly impacts Canadian organizations, who often rely on these hyperscalers. The promise of "sovereign compute" from Ottawa is a good start, but the reality is that demand is outstripping supply globally.

Executives consistently misunderstand that AI adoption is a data problem, not just a technology one. The governance bottleneck is almost always legal review capacity, not model accuracy — the pilot finishes in six weeks and then waits four months. Quebec's Law 25, with its strict transparency requirements for automated decisions, brings this into sharp focus. You cannot explain a black box. If your data lineage is unclear, your AI project is dead on arrival in Quebec.

The trend that worries me is the growing gap between AI experimentation and deployed, governed systems. Many Canadian firms are running pilots, but few are scaling them responsibly. The risk executives are overlooking is not a failed pilot, but a successful one that creates unmanageable compliance exposure or technical debt. The organizations that win will embed legal and risk teams from day one, not at the end of a six-month proof-of-concept.

What can safely wait is investing in the bleeding edge of foundation models. The market is consolidating, as AWS's recent service trimming shows. Focus instead on robust data pipelines and clear governance for the models you already use. The hardest part is organizational change, not buying the next shiny object.

What This Means for Canadian Business

Google Cloud's expanded AI infrastructure and new agent models mean Canadian financial services firms can now deploy more sophisticated AI. These tools allow for automating complex tasks in areas like fraud detection or personalized client interactions. RBC and TD must move beyond basic chatbots to agentic systems that truly transform operations.

Canada's "AI for All" strategy and its compute funding are critical for manufacturing. Firms like CAE and BRP can use affordable sovereign compute to develop AI for predictive maintenance or supply chain optimization. This helps level the playing field against larger international competitors. It also ensures data residency for sensitive industrial processes.

Quebec Law 25's requirements for automated decision transparency create immediate regulatory pressure for all Canadian leaders. Organizations using AI for hiring or credit scoring must ensure they can explain every automated decision. This means General Counsel and Chief Risk Officers need to audit AI systems now to avoid significant penalties.

AI Myth of the Month

Myth

AI adoption is primarily a technology department's responsibility.

Reality

What I've seen inside large enterprises is that successful AI transformation is 80% organizational change and 20% technology. The governance, legal, risk, and change management functions are the true bottlenecks. Handing AI solely to IT ensures pilots remain pilots.
